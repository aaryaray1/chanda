### 2. Against Conceptual Drift in Philosophy of Cosmology
- **Authors:** Philip Rey · **Year:** 2026 · **Venue:** PhilPapers (PhilPapers Foundation) · **Citations:** 0
- **Links:** [doi:10.17605/osf.io/39gst](https://doi.org/10.17605/osf.io/39gst) · [open-access PDF](https://philpapers.org/archive/REYACD.pdf)

> This paper provides a rigorous critical analysis of contemporary debates surrounding the role of non-epistemic values in science, with a specific focus on James Owen Weatherall’s work on physical cosmology (notably concerning Dark Energy and Modified Gravity). The author argues that attempts to introduce "direct" evidential roles for values rest on a fundamental category mistake. By analyzing the "theory-space flexibility" often cited in these debates, the paper demonstrates that what is presented as epistemic sophistication is, in fact, a methodological collapse. The author introduces the concept of "conceptual opportunism"—a procedure in which unstable physical categories are leveraged to generate philosophical significance, only to be dissolved when the requirement for precision becomes inconvenient. The article argues for the restoration of category discipline in the philosophy of physics, showing that local demarcation difficulties do not justify a global skepticism toward the evidence-value distinction. This work serves as a necessary corrective to the "category drift" currently observed in metatheoretical discussions of cosmological models.

### 3. "Hume Variations" by Jerry A. Fodor

- **Links:** [open-access PDF](https://philpapers.org/archive/CRARHV.pdf)

> Contemporary philosophy has had a difficult relationship with its own history. One extreme view conceives of the task of philosophy purely in terms of solving certain given problems, and considers the history of philosophy to have no more relevance to this project than the history of physics has to physics itself. Certainly the history of philosophy is an important intellectual discipline, they argue, but just as physicists do not need to read Newton’s Principia in order to make progress, philosophers do not need to read Aristotle or Kant. At the other extreme are the sceptics who believe that creative philosophy is, for some reason or another it is no longer possible, and that all that can be done is to provide ‘readings’ of the great thinkers of the past. As the late Burton Dreben is supposed to have remarked: ‘junk is junk, but the history of junk is scholarship’.
